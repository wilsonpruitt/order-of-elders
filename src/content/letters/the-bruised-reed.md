> "Here is my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations."
>
> — Isaiah 42:1

Isaiah 42 opens with the first of the four “servant songs.” In these opening verses, Isaiah paints a picture of the servant as someone God personally holds onto and actually enjoys. This servant is steady, Spirit-soaked, and not particularly interested in making a scene. No shouting in the streets. No power plays. Just a quiet confidence that comes from knowing God is near.

The gentleness here is what really gets me. The servant doesn’t snap what’s already bent out of shape or snuff out what’s barely hanging on. She is gentle enough not to break a bruised reed, a weed that’s already been damaged, trampled, beaten, and left to wither in its vulnerability. Instead, she guards what’s fragile until it finds its footing again. Honestly, that alone feels like a spiritual gift in short supply these days.

Gentle doesn’t mean weak. This servant is stubborn in the best possible way. He keeps at God’s justice with patience and grit, refusing to quit when things get hard or discouraging, as they are these days. The servant shines as a sign of God’s promise in the middle of the people, a kind of living covenant. This light spills past Israel’s borders, into dark places, opening blind eyes, loosening chains, and setting people free. God’s justice shows up not with force, but with faithfulness.

All of this is happening as the Babylonian empire is starting to crumble and Cyrus is knocking at the door. The people of God are still in exile. They don’t control their land. They don’t control much of anything, really. In my sacred imagination, I can see them throw up their hands and exclaim, “Someone should do something about this.” Which is a very human impulse, and also a slightly dangerous one.

Scholars remind us that the servant in Second Isaiah isn’t a single heroic figure riding in to save the day. Yes, we can read these verses wearing our Jesus glasses, but there’s also a strong case that the servant is the nation of Israel—the whole people. Further, when Isaiah says the servant won’t cry out or be crushed, that only makes sense if crying out and being crushed would be the normal, expected reaction. In other words, the servant is right in the middle of the mess, right in the middle of the chaos and surrounded by bruised reeds, doing the slow, holy work of justice, hope, and restoration anyway.

This servant song isn’t just about them, back then. It’s also our song. Israel is the servant. Jesus is the servant. And now, like it or not, we, the body of Christ, are the servant. This is our work. Not flashy, not quick-fix work. But faithful, steady, show-up-again-tomorrow work.

This week we celebrate the Baptism of the Lord, and many of our congregations will remember their own baptisms. That might be a good moment to dust off the vows we made—to renounce the spiritual forces of wickedness, reject the evil powers of this world, and resist evil, injustice, and oppression in whatever forms they present themselves.

This is the vocation of God’s servants, and God doesn’t hand us this work and walk away. God is with us, upholding us, delighting in us, breathing the Spirit into what feels bruised and barely burning within us already. And somehow, by grace, that’s enough to keep us going. Beloved, we sing the song, we do the work, and we do it together.
